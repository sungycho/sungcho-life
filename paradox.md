Here are famous paradoxes and counterintuitive effects that reveal how individual actions, incentives, probability, and complex systems can produce unexpected outcomes. Some are strict logical paradoxes; others are well-established economic or social phenomena.

## Economics and incentives

| Paradox                   | Counterintuitive insight                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Jevons’ paradox**       | Making a resource more efficient can increase its total consumption because it becomes cheaper and more useful.                                                       |
| **Paradox of thrift**     | Saving more is prudent for one household, but if everyone saves more during a downturn, demand and total income can fall—leaving society no better able to save.      |
| **Braess’s paradox**      | Adding a road to a traffic network can make everyone’s journey slower because self-interested route choices create a worse equilibrium.                               |
| **Downs–Thomson paradox** | Increasing road capacity can sometimes worsen overall transportation performance by drawing riders away from transit and weakening the transit system.                |
| **Resource curse**        | Countries rich in oil or minerals may experience weaker institutions, more conflict, and slower development than resource-poor countries.                             |
| **Lucas critique**        | A policy relationship observed in historical data may disappear once the policy changes, because people adapt their behavior to the new rules.                        |
| **Goodhart’s law**        | “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” People optimize the metric rather than the underlying goal.                                        |
| **Easterlin paradox**     | Within a country, richer people tend to report greater happiness, yet long-run national income growth does not always produce a comparable rise in average happiness. |

## Collective behavior and strategy

| Paradox                           | Counterintuitive insight                                                                                                                          |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Prisoner’s dilemma**            | Rational choices by each individual can produce an outcome that is worse for everyone than cooperation.                                           |
| **Tragedy of the commons**        | Individually beneficial use of a shared resource can collectively destroy it.                                                                     |
| **Volunteer’s dilemma**           | Everyone benefits if one person bears a cost, but each person prefers that someone else do it—so no one may act.                                  |
| **Abilene paradox**               | A group can collectively choose an outcome that none of its members actually wants because each person incorrectly believes the others prefer it. |
| **Condorcet paradox**             | Even when every voter has consistent preferences, majority voting can produce a cycle: A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A.                       |
| **Arrow’s impossibility theorem** | No ranked-voting system can simultaneously satisfy a set of seemingly reasonable fairness conditions when there are at least three choices.       |
| **Friendship paradox**            | On average, your friends have more friends than you do because highly connected people appear in more people’s friend groups.                     |
| **Tolerance paradox**             | Unlimited tolerance can allow intolerant actors to eliminate the tolerant society itself.                                                         |

## Probability and statistics

| Paradox                    | Counterintuitive insight                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Monty Hall problem**     | After the host reveals a losing door, switching raises the chance of winning from (1/3) to (2/3). New information does not divide the remaining probabilities evenly.                                           |
| **Birthday paradox**       | In a group of only 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday is already above 50%, because there are many possible pairs.                                                                                 |
| **Simpson’s paradox**      | A trend appearing in every subgroup can reverse when the groups are combined, usually because of a lurking variable or unequal group sizes.                                                                     |
| **Base-rate paradox**      | Even a highly accurate test can produce mostly false positives when the condition being tested for is very rare.                                                                                                |
| **Berkson’s paradox**      | Two unrelated characteristics can appear negatively correlated when the sample is selected using a criterion affected by both.                                                                                  |
| **Inspection paradox**     | When you arrive at a bus stop randomly, you are more likely to land in an unusually long gap between buses, so your observed average wait exceeds the schedule’s average interval.                              |
| **St. Petersburg paradox** | A gamble can have an infinite expected monetary value while almost everyone is willing to pay only a modest amount to play, illustrating diminishing marginal utility and the limits of expected value.         |
| **Two-envelope paradox**   | Seemingly valid expected-value reasoning suggests you should always switch envelopes, even though the envelopes are symmetric. The error lies in handling the unknown amounts and probabilities inconsistently. |
| **Boy-or-girl paradox**    | The probability that a two-child family has two boys depends on exactly how you learned that at least one child is a boy. The information-generating process matters.                                           |
| **Lindy effect**           | For some nonperishable things—ideas, books, technologies—the longer they have already survived, the longer they may be expected to survive. Age can signal robustness rather than imminent failure.             |

## Knowledge, observation, and decision-making

| Paradox                                  | Counterintuitive insight                                                                                                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Survivorship bias**                    | Looking only at visible successes can lead you to imitate traits that did not actually cause success while ignoring failures with the same traits.       |
| **Prevention paradox**                   | A preventive measure can create a large benefit for society while offering only a small apparent benefit to each individual.                             |
| **Preparedness paradox**                 | Successful preparation makes a disaster look as though it was never a serious threat, which can undermine support for the preparation that prevented it. |
| **Observer-expectancy effect**           | Expectations can subtly influence measurements, behavior, or interpretation, producing evidence that appears to confirm the original expectation.        |
| **Dunning–Kruger effect**                | Limited knowledge can impair both performance and the ability to recognize one’s poor performance. Greater knowledge may initially reduce confidence.    |
| **Socratic paradox**                     | Recognizing the limits of your knowledge can be a sign of greater wisdom than confidently believing you know more than you do.                           |
| **Paradox of choice**                    | More options can reduce satisfaction and make decisions harder because comparison costs, regret, and expectations increase.                              |
| **Information paradox in organizations** | More reporting and measurement can sometimes reduce understanding by overwhelming attention and encouraging people to focus on visible metrics.          |

## Systems, technology, and organizations

| Paradox                        | Counterintuitive insight                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Automation paradox**         | As systems become more automated, human intervention becomes less frequent but more difficult and consequential when it is required.                                           |
| **Moravec’s paradox**          | Tasks humans find intellectually difficult, such as calculation or chess, can be easy for computers, while ordinary perception and movement can be extremely difficult.        |
| **Productivity paradox**       | Major investments in information technology may not immediately appear in productivity statistics because organizations need time to redesign processes around the technology. |
| **Jevons-like rebound effect** | Energy-efficient cars, appliances, or computing can induce more usage, offsetting some of the expected savings.                                                                |
| **Shirky principle**           | Institutions may preserve the problem they were created to solve because their funding, authority, or identity depends on its continued existence.                             |
| **Conway’s law**               | Organizations tend to design systems that mirror their own communication structures. A fragmented organization often produces fragmented architecture.                         |
| **Gall’s law**                 | Complex systems that work generally evolved from simpler systems that worked; designing a complex system from scratch is unusually likely to fail.                             |
| **Robustness paradox**         | Making a system resistant to common disturbances can cause risks to accumulate or make it more vulnerable to rare, unfamiliar shocks.                                          |

## Logic, language, and philosophy

| Paradox                        | Counterintuitive insight                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Liar paradox**               | “This sentence is false” cannot consistently be classified as either true or false, exposing limits in self-referential systems.                                                |
| **Russell’s paradox**          | The set of all sets that do not contain themselves leads to a contradiction, showing that unrestricted set construction is impossible.                                          |
| **Sorites paradox**            | Removing one grain from a heap does not seem to stop it being a heap, yet repeated removals eventually leave no heap. Vague categories lack precise boundaries.                 |
| **Ship of Theseus**            | If every component of an object is replaced, it is unclear whether it remains the same object, revealing tension between identity, continuity, and composition.                 |
| **Unexpected hanging paradox** | A prediction can appear logically impossible to fulfill, yet the event may still occur unexpectedly; reasoning about future knowledge can alter the conditions being predicted. |
| **Barber paradox**             | A barber who shaves exactly those who do not shave themselves cannot coherently be said either to shave or not shave himself.                                                   |
| **Omnipotence paradox**        | Asking whether an omnipotent being can create an object it cannot control exposes difficulties in defining unlimited power consistently.                                        |
| **Newcomb’s paradox**          | Different compelling decision principles recommend opposite choices, revealing a conflict between causal reasoning and evidential prediction.                                   |

## Science and the physical world

| Paradox                     | Counterintuitive insight                                                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Fermi paradox**           | Given the apparent scale and age of the universe, extraterrestrial civilizations might seem likely—yet we observe no clear evidence of them.                                          |
| **Olbers’ paradox**         | In an eternal, static, infinite universe filled uniformly with stars, the night sky should be bright. Its darkness tells us something fundamental about cosmic history and expansion. |
| **Twin paradox**            | In relativity, a traveling twin can return younger than the stay-at-home twin because the twins follow different paths through spacetime.                                             |
| **Maxwell’s demon**         | A hypothetical being appears able to reduce entropy by sorting molecules, but accounting for information processing restores thermodynamic consistency.                               |
| **Faint young Sun paradox** | The early Sun was weaker, yet geological evidence indicates liquid water existed on Earth, implying compensating atmospheric or planetary effects.                                    |
| **Hydrostatic paradox**     | The pressure at the bottom of a container depends on liquid depth, not the container’s total volume or shape. A narrow column can exert the same bottom pressure as a broad vessel.   |

A useful common theme is:

> **What is locally rational, efficient, or beneficial does not necessarily remain so after behavior, aggregation, selection effects, or feedback loops are taken into account.**

The closest relatives to **Jevons’ paradox** are the rebound effect, Braess’s paradox, the paradox of thrift, Goodhart’s law, the automation paradox, and the preparedness paradox.
